\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A commuter standing on a platform in Olten has a simple question: \emph{Is my connection
reliable? I have four minutes.} Public transport operators publish punctuality figures that
seem to answer it. In Switzerland these figures rest on a crisp rule: a train counts as
punctual if it arrives less than three minutes late, and the share of punctual trains is
reported for the network as a whole. Such a figure is precise, but it answers a different
question. It does not know which train the commuter arrives with, which train they want to
catch, or how many minutes they have to change. A train that is two minutes late is
punctual by the official rule and still makes the commuter miss a connection with a
two-minute transfer. Passenger research points in the same direction: passengers judge
``on time'' more strictly than the industry standard and expect a train to arrive within
about one minute of the timetable \citep{transportfocus2015}.

A natural interface for such questions is a conversational assistant. Large language models
can turn tables of delay statistics into fluent answers such as ``most of your trains are
slightly late''. Whether such sentences are \emph{true} for the data is a separate matter.
Recent work checks quantified statements written by language models against their source
tables with crisp truth conditions, for example ``most'' meaning ``more than half''
\citep{eida2026}. Everyday quantifiers and adjectives, however, are vague: ``most'',
``about half'' and ``slightly late'' hold to a degree. A crisp checker that declares ``most
trains are late'' true as soon as a bare majority of trains is late cannot see the
borderline cases in which a commuter would disagree.

Fuzzy linguistic summaries compute such sentences, together with their degree of truth,
directly from data \citep{yager1982,kacprzyk2009}. A summary such as ``few of your
connections at Olten are missed'' has a truth value in $[0, 1]$ that can be recomputed from
the measurements at any time. Quality measures add the questions of how much data a sentence
rests on and how well it holds in each region \citep{hudec2018,hudec2022}. Linguistic
summaries have been mined from road traffic data in the Human-IST line of work
\citep{pincay2021}, which names the combination with quality measures and the regional view
as future work. We take that step for public transport, and we put the summaries into the
hands of a citizen: the summaries are not an add-on that describes an opaque decision, they
\emph{are} the decision.

This paper presents a \textbf{Swiss public-transport assistant}. Its answer to the
commuter's question consists of a decision word (\emph{safe}, \emph{at risk} or
\emph{risky}) and an explanation made of fuzzy linguistic summaries, computed from the
operators' published actual arrival and departure times (Ist-Daten, published on
opentransportdata.swiss). Because the decision word and the explanation sentences come from
the same truth computation, the explanation is faithful by construction: every number in it
can be recomputed from the data, and the assistant checks this for every answer it gives.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq}
The study is organised around one main research question and three sub-questions, each
phrased as a question a citizen might ask. The hypotheses, their tests and their decision
rules were registered before any real data was analysed (Section~\ref{sec:study}).

\begin{description}
  \item[Main RQ.] Can fuzzy linguistic summaries be the explainable core of a
  citizen-facing assistant that answers questions about the reliability of Swiss
  public-transport trips?
  \item[SRQ1, ``Is the answer about my trip?''] With coverage and regional quality measures
  (Hudec), which summaries still hold for a citizen's own line/station/time, and how often
  do network-wide summaries mask local reality?
  \item[SRQ2, ``At risk for whom?''] How often does the crisp official view give a
  different connection verdict than a perception-aware view based on the passenger's
  transfer margin?
  \item[SRQ3, ``Can I trust the chatbot?''] Are LLM-written quantified claims faithful to
  the data, does fuzzy grounding help, and where do crisp and fuzzy checkers disagree?
\end{description}

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{computing-with-words engine} that mines fuzzy linguistic summaries with
  Hudec's coverage and quality measures from public Swiss operational data, with the
  configuration of \citet{pincay2021} as a methodological baseline.
  \item An \textbf{explainable assistant} whose decision word and explanation come from the
  same truth computation, with a \emph{perception-aware} notion of ``late'' that depends on
  the passenger's own time to change trains, and with the crisp official view next to it.
  \item An \textbf{audit of language-model answers} that extends crisp verification of
  quantified statements \citep{eida2026} to vague quantifiers, graded truth and real data,
  and compares a crisp and a fuzzy checker on the same claims.
\end{enumerate}
